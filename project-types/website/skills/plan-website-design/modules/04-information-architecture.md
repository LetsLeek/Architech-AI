# Information Architecture

## Purpose

Organize the canonical website requirements and relevant customer information
into a coherent information hierarchy for each design proposal before choosing
concrete pages, routes, navigation targets, or section trees.

Information architecture determines how information is grouped, prioritized,
related, and exposed. It does not itself create new customer requirements.

## Requirement-Led Structure

Use website requirements as the primary scope boundary.

Mandatory requirements must have a clear conceptual place in every proposal.

Should requirements should normally be represented.

Could requirements may be treated differently across proposals when their
optional status permits.

Do not create a dedicated page merely because a requirement exists.

## Relevant Customer Information

Use canonical customer information where it supports required content,
reasonable presentation, or the proposal's information structure.

Do not force every available customer-profile fact into the website.

Existing customer facts may support lightweight presentation decisions,
but must not be expanded into unsupported functionality or substantial
new content scope.

## Information Grouping

Group information according to meaningful user and content relationships.

Examples may include relationships between:

- offerings and pricing;
- location and opening hours;
- contact information and conversion paths;
- gallery content and brand storytelling;
- testimonials and trust-supporting information.

Presentation groups are design decisions and must not be represented as
new customer business facts.

## Information Hierarchy

Determine which information should be primary, supporting, secondary,
or utility-oriented according to goals, target audiences, requirements,
constraints, and the proposal direction.

Do not hide or weaken mandatory customer requirements for the sake of
proposal differentiation.

## Functional Relationships

Place functional requirements in the information context where they can
be understood and accessed coherently.

Do not invent new functionality while organizing existing requirements.

A goal may influence the prominence of an existing contact or booking path
but does not independently create such a function.

## Structural Tendencies

Use the established design direction to determine a suitable structural tendency.

A proposal may favor a compact, multi-page, or hybrid experience where the
requirements and design boundaries permit.

Do not finalize concrete pages, routes, navigation labels, or navigation targets
in this phase.

## Intentional Repetition

Information may be intentionally exposed in more than one place when doing so
improves discoverability or supports a different presentation purpose.

Avoid uncontrolled duplication of complete content structures.

Canonical customer data remains the source of truth wherever reused.

## Unknowns and Conflicts

Accommodate unresolved information without inventing or selecting definitive facts.

Do not use information architecture to silently resolve conflicting customer data.

Prefer structures that remain valid when unresolved canonical information is
later clarified.

## Cross-Proposal Variation

Allow the three proposals to organize and prioritize the same requirement basis
differently when the permitted design space allows.

Variation may occur in grouping, hierarchy, emphasis, and structural tendency.

Do not achieve structural variation by dropping mandatory scope or inventing
new customer needs.

## Coverage Check

Before continuing, confirm for each proposal that every mandatory requirement
has a conceptual placement and that optional scope has been handled consistently
with its supplied strength.

Do not create a separate information-architecture output artifact.

## Handoff

After establishing the information hierarchy and grouping for each proposal,
continue to Pages and Navigation Planning.

Translate the conceptual information architecture into concrete pages, routes,
navigation groups, and navigation targets.
